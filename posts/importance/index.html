Importance sampling ⚖️

June 2023, reworked in 2026

Sampling refers to the generation of random variables following a certain probability distribution; for example if FFF is a density on Rd\mathbb{R}^dRd, we want to generate random variables which are independent and follow the distribution given by FFF, or we want to compute expectations like E[φ(X)]\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]E[φ(X)] for some function φ\varphiφ, where X∼FX \sim FX∼F. In many cases, one does not fully knows FFF, but only that it is proportional to some function fff, that is

F(x)=f(x)ZfwhereZf=∫f(u)du,F(x) = \frac{f(x)}{Z_f} \qquad \text{where} \qquad Z_f = \int f(u)du,F(x)=Zf​f(x)​whereZf​=∫f(u)du,

and computing the normalizing constant ZfZ_fZf​ is intractable.

There are many techniques that still allow to sample from FFF in this case; the whole field of Monte-Carlo research consists in crafting stochastic systems (Markov Chains, diffusions) that converge toward samples from FFF. In this note I'm focusing on a simpler method, importance sampling (IS), also called reweighting, which allows to compute integrals like above, without sampling from FFF. We will see an insightful result by Chatterjee and Diaconis on the number of samples required for IS to be sufficiently precise.

The basic idea of Importance Sampling

Let GGG be another probability density, supposed easy to sample from. Throughout, F,GF,GF,G denote the normalized densities and f,gf,gf,g their unnormalized versions, so that F=f/ZfF = f/Z_fF=f/Zf​ and G=g/ZgG = g/Z_gG=g/Zg​. We will never use the exact values of ZfZ_fZf​ or ZgZ_gZg​.

In the sequel, we will adopt the following notations.

  • Samples from FFF are noted XXX or XiX_iXi​. The density FFF is called the target.

  • Samples from GGG are noted YYY or YiY_iYi​. The density GGG is called the proposal.

  • The ratio of unnormalized densities will be denoted by w(x)=f(x)/g(x)w(x) = f(x)/g(x)w(x)=f(x)/g(x).

  • The ratio of the densities will be denoted by W(x)=F(x)/G(x)W(x) = F(x)/G(x)W(x)=F(x)/G(x).

Then, for any function φ\varphiφ,

E[φ(Y)w(Y)]=∫φ(y)f(y)g(y)G(y)dy=1Zg∫φ(y)f(y)dy=ZfZgE[φ(X)].\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}[\varphi(Y)w(Y)] &= \int \varphi(y)\frac{f(y)}{g(y)}G(y)dy \\ &= \frac{1}{Z_g}\int \varphi(y)f(y)dy \\ &= \frac{Z_f}{Z_g} \mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]. \end{aligned}E[φ(Y)w(Y)]​=∫φ(y)g(y)f(y)​G(y)dy=Zg​1​∫φ(y)f(y)dy=Zg​Zf​​E[φ(X)].​

Applying this formula to φ≡1\varphi \equiv 1φ≡1 yields E[w(Y)]=Zf/Zg\mathbb{E}[w(Y)] =Z_f/Z_gE[w(Y)]=Zf​/Zg​. Consequently,

E[φ(Y)w(Y)]E[w(Y)]=E[φ(X)].\frac{ \mathbb{E}[\varphi(Y)w(Y)] }{ \mathbb{E}[w(Y)] } = \mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)].E[w(Y)]E[φ(Y)w(Y)]​=E[φ(X)].

This suggests the following method for approximating E[φ(X)]\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]E[φ(X)] without computing ZfZ_fZf​ or ZgZ_gZg​.

Importance Sampling. Let Y1,…,YnY_1, \dotsc, Y_nY1​,…,Yn​ be iid with density GGG. Then, when n→∞n\to\inftyn→∞, almost surely one has ∑i=1nw(Yi)n→ZfZg \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w(Y_i)}{n} \to \frac{Z_f}{Z_g} n∑i=1n​w(Yi​)​→Zg​Zf​​ and ∑i=1nw(Yi)φ(Yi)∑i=1nw(Yi)→E[φ(X)]=∫φ(x)F(x)dx. \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w(Y_i) \varphi(Y_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^n w(Y_i)} \to \mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]= \int \varphi(x)F(x)dx.∑i=1n​w(Yi​)∑i=1n​w(Yi​)φ(Yi​)​→E[φ(X)]=∫φ(x)F(x)dx.

Proof. By the Law of Large Numbers, the LHS of (4) converges towards E[w(Y)]=∫w(y)G(y)dy=Zf/Zg\mathbb{E}[w(Y)] = \int w(y)G(y)dy = Z_f/Z_gE[w(Y)]=∫w(y)G(y)dy=Zf​/Zg​.

Also by the LLN, ∑w(Yi)φ(Yi)n\frac{\sum w(Y_i)\varphi(Y_i)}{n}n∑w(Yi​)φ(Yi​)​ converges towards E[φ(Y)w(Y)]=(Zf/Zg)E[φ(X)]\mathbb{E}[\varphi(Y)w(Y)] = (Z_f/Z_g)\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]E[φ(Y)w(Y)]=(Zf​/Zg​)E[φ(X)].

Take the ratio of the two to get (5).

This technique explains the word importance sampling: the samples YiY_iYi​ are iid but their density is G(y)G(y)G(y), not F(y)F(y)F(y), and the weights correct the difference between the two. If some yyy is very likely under GGG but not so much under FFF (meaning that F(y)F(y)F(y) is close to zero but G(y)G(y)G(y) is high), then this sample will be assigned a very small weight.

How good is this approximation?

Unless FFF and GGG are already very close to each other, this estimator can be very bad. Let's look at the variance of the estimator Z^\hat{Z}Z^ given in (4): clearly E[Z^]=Zf/Zg\mathbb{E}[\hat{Z}]=Z_f/Z_gE[Z^]=Zf​/Zg​, hence

Var(Z^)=∫Gf2g2−(Zf/Zg)2n=∫gZgf2g2−(Zf/Zg)2n=(Zf/Zg)E[w(X)]−(Zf/Zg)2n.\begin{aligned} \mathrm{Var}(\hat{Z}) &= \frac{\int G\frac{f^2}{g^2} - (Z_f/Z_g)^2}{n} \\ &= \frac{\int \frac{g}{Z_g} \frac{f^2}{g^2} - (Z_f/Z_g)^2}{n} \\ &= \frac{(Z_f/Z_g)\mathbb{E}[w(X)]-(Z_f/Z_g)^2}{n}. \end{aligned}Var(Z^)​=n∫Gg2f2​−(Zf​/Zg​)2​=n∫Zg​g​g2f2​−(Zf​/Zg​)2​=n(Zf​/Zg​)E[w(X)]−(Zf​/Zg​)2​.​

The first term, especially E[w(X)]\mathbb{E}[w(X)]E[w(X)], could be prohibitively big. Indeed, let us consider the following situation: our prior density is a Standard Gaussian G(x)=e−x2/2/2πG(x) = e^{-x^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}G(x)=e−x2/2/2π​, and our target density is the same, but shifted by 10: F(x)=e−(x−10)2/2/2πF(x) = e^{-(x-10)^2/2}/\sqrt{2\pi}F(x)=e−(x−10)2/2/2π​. Here both densities are normalized, so Zf=Zg=1Z_f=Z_g=1Zf​=Zg​=1 and f=Ff=Ff=F, g=Gg=Gg=G. But,

E[F(X)/G(X)]=12π∫e−(x−10)22e−(x−10)22+x22dx=12π∫e−(x−20)22+100dx=e100.\begin{aligned}\mathbb{E}[F(X)/G(X)] &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{-\frac{(x-10)^2}{2}}e^{-\frac{(x-10)^2 }{ 2} + \frac{x^2}{2}}dx\\ &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int e^{- \frac{(x-20)^2}{2} + 100}dx \\&= e^{100}.\end{aligned}E[F(X)/G(X)]​=2π​1​∫e−2(x−10)2​e−2(x−10)2​+2x2​dx=2π​1​∫e−2(x−20)2​+100dx=e100.​

That is too big. Having a low variance for Z^\hat{Z}Z^ would require having more than e100e^{100}e100 samples, which is impossible. And indeed, one can even find simple examples (exponential distributions) for which the variance is infinite.

The Effective Sample Size

Practically, a good indicator of the quality of our importance sampling is given by the effective sample size.

Suppose you used nnn samples YiY_iYi​ from GGG. If they were distributed exactly as FFF, that is if ggg was proportional to fff, we would have w(Yi)=Zf/Zgw(Y_i) = Z_f/Z_gw(Yi​)=Zf​/Zg​ for all YiY_iYi​. In general fff is not proportional to ggg hence that's not the case and one way to measure how far the samples we have are from nnn samples of FFF, we set σ^n\hat{\sigma}_nσ^n​ = the empirical coefficient of variation of the w(Yi)w(Y_i)w(Yi​), and

ESS(n)=n1+σ^n. \mathrm{ESS}(n) = \frac{n}{1 + \hat{\sigma}_n}.ESS(n)=1+σ^n​n​.

How to use the ESS ?

Suppose that you use IS with a sample size of n=1000n=1000n=1000. If ESS≈1000\mathrm{ESS} \approx 1000ESS≈1000 then the weigths are almost constant and there's a good chance that our sampling is excellent. If it's small, say ESS≈100\mathrm{ESS} \approx 100ESS≈100, then it means that the quality of your IS estimator is the same as if you would have used 100 samples of the real distribution FFF.

This method is a good rule of thumb to assess the quality of IS, but it's quite empirical. In general, what is the required number of samples when one wants to efficiently estimate a mean E[φ(X)]\mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]E[φ(X)] using importance sampling?

The number of samples required for IS

For any function φ\varphiφ, we will denote by

Jn(φ)=∑i=1nw(Yi)φ(Yi)∑i=1nw(Yi)J_n(\varphi) = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n w(Y_i)\varphi(Y_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^n w(Y_i)}Jn​(φ)=∑i=1n​w(Yi​)∑i=1n​w(Yi​)φ(Yi​)​

Note that JnJ_nJn​ is unchanged if the weights w=f/gw=f/gw=f/g are replaced by the normalized likelihood ratio W=F/GW = F/GW=F/G; in the sequel of this section we work with WWW, for which E[W(Y)]=1\mathbb{E}[W(Y)]=1E[W(Y)]=1.

The Kullback-Leibler divergence is

It is supposed to be <∞<\infty<∞. It can also be defined as D=E[log⁡W(X)]D=\mathbb{E}[\log W(X)]D=E[logW(X)]. In general, concentration inequalities allow to bound how much log⁡W(X)\log W(X)logW(X) is concentrated around its mean DDD: depending on F,GF,GF,G, the deviation probability

ϵ(s)=P(∣log⁡W(X)−D∣>s)\epsilon(s)=\mathbb{P}(|\log W(X) - D| > s)ϵ(s)=P(∣logW(X)−D∣>s)

is a decreasing function of sss with ϵ(s)→0\epsilon(s)\to 0ϵ(s)→0. The error terms in the sequel will be expressed using this function ϵ\epsilonϵ. For any positive sss we note

ε(s)=(e−s/4+2ϵ(s/2))1/2.\varepsilon(s) = (e^{-s/4} + 2\sqrt{\epsilon(s/2)})^{1/2}.ε(s)=(e−s/4+2ϵ(s/2)​)1/2.

Theorem (Chatterjee and Diaconis, 2015).

Positive part. Suppose that n=eD+sn=e^{D+s}n=eD+s. Then, for any φ\varphiφ which is in L2(F)L^2(F)L2(F), P(∣Jn(φ)−E[φ(X)]∣>4ε(s)∣φ∣2)⩽2ε(s).\mathbb{P}(|J_n(\varphi) - \mathbb{E}[\varphi(X)]|>4\varepsilon(s) |\varphi|_2)\leqslant 2\varepsilon(s).P(∣Jn​(φ)−E[φ(X)]∣>4ε(s)∣φ∣2​)⩽2ε(s). To summarize: if one has more than eDe^{D}eD samples, then for every fixed φ\varphiφ the probability of having Jn(φ)J_n(\varphi)Jn​(φ) close to I(φ)I(\varphi)I(φ) is high. But if nnn is smaller than eDe^DeD, then there might be functions such that with high probability, the estimator Jn(φ)J_n(\varphi)Jn​(φ) is far away from I(φ)I(\varphi)I(φ). The original proof in the Chatterjee-Diaconis paper shows that the function φ(x)=1W(x)<λ\varphi(x) = \mathbb{1}_{W(x)<\lambda}φ(x)=1W(x)<λ​ is itself hard to approximate, for some λ\lambdaλ. That the KL itself is hard to estimate is due to El Houssain Chahboun, a young PhD student under the supervision of Pierre Jacob. The proof is entirely due to him!

Uniform bounds. Note that for a specific φ\varphiφ, it might very well be true that less than eDe^DeD samples are needed. But eDe^DeD is always enough. This asks for a natural question: how many samples do you need so that every estimator of every φ\varphiφ is simultaneously good? That is, could we control, for example,

sup⁡φ∈F∣Jn(φ)−I(φ)∣\sup_{\varphi \in \mathscr{F}}\left|J_n(\varphi) - I(\varphi)\right|φ∈Fsup​∣Jn​(φ)−I(φ)∣

F^n=∑i=1nwiδYi.\hat{F}_n = \sum_{i=1}^n w_i\delta_{Y_i}.F^n​=i=1∑n​wi​δYi​​.

In a recent work with Michael Goldmann, we computed the asymptotics of W1(F,F^n)W_1(F, \hat{F}_n)W1​(F,F^n​) as nnn is large[1].

Wasserstein distances. For F,GF,GF,G compactly supported and bounded away from zero and ∞\infty∞, we have Wpd(F^n,F)≍n−p/dCp(F∣G)W_p^d(\hat{F}_n,F)\asymp n^{-p/d}\mathscr{C}_p(F\mid G)Wpd​(F^n​,F)≍n−p/dCp​(F∣G), where

So a good proxy for Chatterjee and Diaconis’ result would be that, to have a good approximation, we would need a number nnn of samples at least comparable to cdc^dcd, where c=C1(G∣F)c = \mathscr{C}_1(G\mid F)c=C1​(G∣F). If ddd is large, we can work further our approximation:

That is equal to exp⁡(D−∫Flog⁡F)\exp(D - \int F\log F)exp(D−∫FlogF), which is not the eDe^DeD of Chatterjee-Diaconis. When it is very largely positive, it means that the entropy is largely negative and hence that FFF is very concentrated.
