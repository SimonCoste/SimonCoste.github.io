Importance sampling ⚖️

June 2023, reworked in 2026

Sampling refers to the generation of random variables following a certain probability distribution; for example if FFF is a density on Rd\mathbb{R}^dRd, we want to generate random variables which are independent and follow the distribution given by FFF, or we want to compute expectations like

EX∼F[φ(X)]=∫φ(x)F(x)dx\mathbb{E}_{X \sim F}[\varphi(X)] = \int \varphi(x)F(x)dxEX∼F​[φ(X)]=∫φ(x)F(x)dx

for some function φ\varphiφ. In many cases, one does not fully knows FFF, but only that it is proportional to some function fff, that is

F(x)=f(x)ZfwhereZf=∫f(u)du,F(x) = \frac{f(x)}{Z_f} \qquad \text{where} \qquad Z_f = \int f(u)du,F(x)=Zf​f(x)​whereZf​=∫f(u)du,

and computing the normalizing constant ZfZ_fZf​ is intractable.

There are many techniques that still allow to sample from FFF in this case; the whole field of Monte-Carlo research consists in crafting stochastic systems (Markov Chains, diffusions) that converge toward samples from FFF. In this note I'm focusing on a simpler method, importance sampling (IS), also called reweighting, which allows to compute integrals like above, without sampling from FFF.

Plan of the note.

  • First, I define IS and give examples.

  • Second, I introduce a usefull metric called the Effective Sample Size. Statisticians know this stuff very well.

  • Then I explain see how much compute you need to use IS efficiently: first with a result by Chatterjee and Diaconis for estimating a specific integral, then a result on Wasserstein distances for estimating simultaneously every integral.

  • There's a small section of "useful" diagnostics for IS estimators.

  • And then there's the proof of the Chatterjee-Diaconis result.

The basic idea of Importance Sampling

Let GGG be another probability density, supposed easy to sample from. Throughout, F,GF,GF,G denote the normalized densities and f,gf,gf,g their unnormalized versions, so that F=f/ZfF = f/Z_fF=f/Zf​ and G=g/ZgG = g/Z_gG=g/Zg​. We will never use the exact values of ZfZ_fZf​ or ZgZ_gZg​.

In the sequel, we will adopt the following notations.

  • Samples from FFF are noted XXX or XiX_iXi​. The density FFF is called the target.

  • Samples from GGG are noted YYY or YiY_iYi​. The density GGG is called the proposal.

  • The ratio of unnormalized densities will be denoted by w(x)=f(x)/g(x)w(x) = f(x)/g(x)w(x)=f(x)/g(x).

  • This technique explains the word importance sampling: the samples YiY_iYi​ are iid but their density is G(y)G(y)G(y), not F(y)F(y)F(y), and the weights correct the difference between the two.

  • The estimator in (6) is sometimes called Self-Normalized Importance Sampling (SNIS), because of the normalization term Z^n=(w1+⋯+wn)/n\hat{Z}_n = (w_1+\dotsb + w_n)/nZ^n​=(w1​+⋯+wn​)/n.

How good is this approximation?

Unless FFF and GGG are already very close to each other, this estimator can be very bad. Let's look at the variance of the estimator Z^\hat{Z}Z^ given in (5): clearly E[Z^]=Zf/Zg\mathbb{E}[\hat{Z}]=Z_f/Z_gE[Z^]=Zf​/Zg​, hence

The first term, especially E[w(X)]\mathbb{E}[w(X)]E[w(X)], could be prohibitively big. The Effective Sample Size

The ESS. Let us note wˉi\bar{w}_iwˉi​ the normalized weights,

wˉi=w(yi)∑i=1nw(Yi).\bar{w}_i = \frac{w(y_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^n w(Y_i)}.wˉi​=∑i=1n​w(Yi​)w(yi​)​.

The SNIS estimation of ∫φF\int \varphi F∫φF is

Jn=∑wˉiφ(Yi).J_n = \sum \bar{w}_i \varphi(Y_i).Jn​=∑wˉi​φ(Yi​).

Suppose for a moment that you are in the ideal case where your proposal distribution GGG is equal to FFF, and you have mmm samples YiY_iYi​. Then, all the weights w(Yi)w(Y_i)w(Yi​) are equal to 1 and thus wˉi=1/m\bar{w}_i = 1/mwˉi​=1/m. The variance of Jm{J}_mJm​ in this case is simply

σ2m\frac{\sigma^2}{m}mσ2​

where σ2=Var(φ(Y))\sigma^2 = \mathrm{Var}(\varphi(Y))σ2=Var(φ(Y)). Now go back to the case where you have nnn samples YiY_iYi​ (I'll call them "fake samples") from a proposal distribution GGG. Forget an instant that the wˉi\bar{w}_iwˉi​ are random, and treat them like fixed weights. Yes, I know they're not, but imagine. Then the variance of JnJ_nJn​ should be

∑i=1wˉi2Var(φ(Y))=∣wˉ∣22σ2.\sum_{i=1}\bar{w}_i^2 \mathrm{Var}(\varphi(Y)) = |\bar{w}|_2^2 \sigma^2.i=1∑​wˉi2​Var(φ(Y))=∣wˉ∣22​σ2.

This is actually a good approximation of the variance of JnJ_nJn​, as explained in the litterature (e.g. in Kong's seminal paper or in Liu's paper).

Given these two approximations, what is the number of "real" samples from FFF that would give the same variance as nnn "fake" samples? Just solve (11) = (12) and you get the Effective Sample Size,

n∗=1∑i=1nwˉi2.n_* = \frac{1}{\sum_{i=1}^n \bar{w}_i^2}.n∗​=∑i=1n​wˉi2​1​.

Other equivalent forms are sometimes seen in the litterature, e.g. in Kong's seminal paper[1], with more rigorous derivations (they consist in using the "delta-method" to estimate Var(Jn)\mathrm{Var}(J_n)Var(Jn​) and are not especially enlightening).

An ESS of n∗=100n_*=100n∗​=100 with a sample size of 1000 says that your 1000 "fake samples" will get you the same precision as 100 "real samples". Ultimately, if one real sample from FFF costs 1€, then the real price of your nnn fake samples would be n∗n_*n∗​€. And we always have n∗⩽nn_* \leqslant nn∗​⩽n, of course.

Generalized ESS. In general, if you have a decent "precision metric" to evaluate an estimator, you can compute a generalized notion of sample size: just compute the metric on your nnn samples, and then compute the number mmm of real samples which would give the same metric.

In practice, the ESS is not really used to assess the quality of an estimator, but rather to take decisions on when to resample or not. It is often used in sequential settings, where one can monitor the ESS along time and decide to resample the proposals when the ESS collapses.

However, Empirical Sample Sizes often do not tell the whole story, and are not very informative on the precision of an estimator, but rather on the relative precision compared to other estimators. Concretely, they do not tell you if your nnn samples are really enough to estimate ∫φF\int \varphi F∫φF.

The Informational sample size

The Kullback-Leibler divergence from FFF towards GGG is

D=dKL(F∣G)=∫Flog⁡(F/G).D = d_{\mathrm{KL}}(F\mid G) = \int F \log (F/G).D=dKL​(F∣G)=∫Flog(F/G).

To summarize: if one has more than eDe^{D}eD samples, then for every fixed φ\varphiφ the probability of having Jn(φ)J_n(\varphi)Jn​(φ) close to I(φ)I(\varphi)I(φ) is high. The Wasserstein sample size

If F\mathscr{F}F is the set of all 1-Lipschitz functions, it turns out that this supremum is nothing more than the Wasserstein-1 distance between the target density FFF and the « empirical self-normalized weighted measure », namely

F^n=∑i=1nwˉiδYi\hat{F}_n = \sum_{i=1}^n \bar{w}_i\delta_{Y_i}F^n​=i=1∑n​wˉi​δYi​​

where wˉi=w(Yi)/(w(Y1)+⋯+w(Yn))\bar{w}_i = w(Y_i) / (w(Y_1)+\dotsb + w(Y_n))wˉi​=w(Yi​)/(w(Y1​)+⋯+w(Yn​)) are the self-normalized weights. In a recent work with Michael Goldmann, we computed the asymptotics of W1(F,F^n)W_1(F, \hat{F}_n)W1​(F,F^n​) as nnn is large[2].

Wasserstein asymptotics. For F,GF,GF,G compactly supported and bounded away from zero and ∞\infty∞, we have

E[Wpp(F^n,F)]≍n−p/d∫FG−p/d.\mathbb{E}[W_p^p(\hat{F}_n,F)]\asymp n^{-p/d}\int F G^{-p/d}.E[Wpp​(F^n​,F)]≍n−p/d∫FG−p/d.

  • Here, ≍\asymp≍ means that the LHS is between c1×LHSc_1 \times LHSc1​×LHS and c2×LHSc_2 \times LHSc2​×LHS, for some unknosn absolute constants c1⩽c2c_1 \leqslant c_2c1​⩽c2​. However, in the p=2p=2p=2 case, it is known that c1=c2c_1=c_2c1​=c2​, so that the result is actually a true limit.

  • You can safely remove the E\mathbb{E}E. We didn't include it in the paper we all you have to check is a concentration bound. It's safe.

Consequence on the sample size. Using this asymptotic, a good proxy for Chatterjee and Diaconis’ study would be that, to have a good approximation, we would need a number nnn of samples at least comparable to cdc^dcd, where c=∫FG−p/dc = \int F G^{-p/d}c=∫FG−p/d. If ddd is large, we can work further our approximation:

cd=(∫Fe−1dlog⁡G)d≈(∫F(1−d−1log⁡G))d≈(1−1d∫Flog⁡G)d≈e−∫Flog⁡G.\begin{aligned} c^d &= \left(\int F e^{-\frac{1}{d}\log G}\right)^d \\ &\approx \left(\int F(1 - d^{-1}\log G)\right)^d \\ &\approx\left(1 - \frac{1}{d}\int F \log G\right)^d \\ &\approx e^{-\int F \log G}. \end{aligned}cd​=(∫Fe−d1​logG)d≈(∫F(1−d−1logG))d≈(1−d1​∫FlogG)d≈e−∫FlogG.​

The entropy of FFF is E=−∫Flog⁡FE = -\int F \log FE=−∫FlogF; therefore, the term above is also equal to exp⁡(D−∫Flog⁡F)=exp⁡(D+E)\exp(D - \int F\log F)= \exp(D+E)exp(D−∫FlogF)=exp(D+E), which is not the eDe^DeD of Chatterjee-Diaconis. What happens here is not 100% clear for me, but here is my take:

  • Low entropy means less samples are needed. If EEE is very low, then FFF is very concentrated; ultimately one can think of FFF as almost a Dirac located at a point, say 000. Then, all the integrals ∫φF\int \varphi F∫φF are either 000 (if the support of φ\varphiφ avoids zero) or ≈φ(0)\approx \varphi(0)≈φ(0). If there is a proposal point close to 0, then ALL the integrals will be well approximated. Hence the need for less points.

  • High entropy means more samples are needed. If EEE is high then FFF is more uniform on its support. But then, will high probability there will be a (random) zone in the domain where there is a lack of points, and the functions supported on this zone will be badly approximated. Hence the need for more points.

Diagnoses

As explained in the ESS section, a prominent question in the theory of IS is: how can we craft diagnostic metrics of an estimator, that indicate whether the estimator is good or bad?

A priori, the two results given above could give an answer: we have to check if nnn is greater than, for example, eDe^DeD. We do not know DDD but we could still estimate it using IS, with

D^n=∑w(Yi)log⁡w(Yi)∑w(Yi)−log⁡Z^n.\hat{D}_n = \frac{\sum w(Y_i)\log w(Y_i)}{\sum w(Y_i)} - \log \hat{Z}_n.D^n​=∑w(Yi​)∑w(Yi​)logw(Yi​)​−logZ^n​.

Then declare your IS estimator to be good is n>eD^nn > e^{\hat{D}_n}n>eD^n​.

This is dommed to fail. As Chatterjee and Diaconis explain, "any diagnostic criterion that is itself dependent on the accuracy of an estimate obtained by importance sampling, is unlikely to be effective as a measure of the efficacy of importance sampling". Id est, the second point of the theorem tells you that estimating DDD with (23) is already a bad idea, so don't use it to estimate if nnn is large enough.

That's the point of the ESS: it is self-contained, in that you only need the weights w(yi)w(y_i)w(yi​) to compute the diagnostic criterion. But the ESS itself is a weak criterion, in that there is no connection between the variance of www and eDe^DeD. This is why Chatterjee and Diaconis favor another criterion, which is simply the L∞/L1L^\infty / L^1L∞/L1 ratio,

Qn=max⁡w(yi)∑w(yi).Q_n = \frac{\max w(y_i)}{\sum w(y_i)}.Qn​=∑w(yi​)maxw(yi​)​.

They have convincing but incomplete heuristics on this diagnostic quantity. I will, at some point, expose them here.
